ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS
Customer churn predictor, third assignment


1. WHAT THE SYSTEM DECIDES

The backend serves a logistic regression that estimates the probability a
Telco customer will cancel. A prediction of 1 flags the customer, and in
practice a flag triggers a retention action: a call, a discount, a contract
offer.

That framing decides everything else in this report. Being flagged is a
benefit here, not a penalty. A false positive gives a discount to someone who
was staying anyway, which costs the company money and leaves the customer
better off. A false negative lets a customer leave without ever being offered
anything.

If the flag triggered something else, for example higher prices, lower support
priority, or being written off as already lost, the sign of every argument
below would flip. The model does not know what the flag is used for, and
nothing in the API records it. That is the first ethical gap: the system is
deployed without its purpose being written down anywhere a user can see.


2. THE THRESHOLD IS THE REAL DECISION

The model produces a probability. Turning that into a yes or no needs a cut,
and the cut is a policy choice, not a technical one. There are two defensible criteria
disagree by a wide margin:

  threshold            flagged    missed churners   wasted offers    F1
  0.500 (default)         1175                142             569   0.630
  0.584 (best F1)          987                197             436   0.635
  0.354 (lowest cost)     1538                 71             861   0.592

Moving from the F1 threshold to the cost threshold flags 551 more customers,
rescues 126 who would otherwise have been missed, and hands 425 discounts to
people who were not leaving.

Neither is correct. Maximising F1 treats a lost customer and a wasted discount
as equally bad, which nobody actually believes. The cost threshold assumes a
lost customer is five times worse, which is a number I picked with no data
behind it. The honest description is that the app lets a human choose between
two arbitrary weightings, and the weighting is the ethical content.

Exposing the threshold as a slider is better than hiding it, because it makes
the choice visible instead of burying 0.5 in a library default. But the
implementation has two problems. The threshold is global server state, so one
person dragging the slider changes every prediction every other caller
receives. And nothing records who changed it, when, or why. A decision that
moves 551 customers in or out of a retention programme leaves no trace.


3. WHO GETS FLAGGED

Measured on the test set at threshold 0.584. TPR is the share of churners
correctly flagged, FPR the share of non-churners wrongly flagged, PPV the
share of flagged customers who really churn.

                       n    churn rate   flagged     TPR     FPR     PPV
  gender F          1434         0.268     0.349   0.727   0.211   0.557
  gender M          1384         0.263     0.351   0.747   0.210   0.560

  Senior no         2352         0.237     0.308   0.709   0.184   0.545
  Senior yes         466         0.410     0.562   0.817   0.385   0.595

  Dependents no     1969         0.315     0.432   0.775   0.274   0.566
  Dependents yes     849         0.150     0.161   0.551   0.093   0.511

Gender shows no disparity worth discussing. The rates match to within a
percentage point, which is consistent with the exploratory analysis, where
churn was 26.9 percent for women and 26.2 percent for men.

Age and family status are a different matter. A senior citizen is flagged
56.2 percent of the time against 30.8 percent for everyone else. Most of that
follows the base rate, since seniors churn at 41.0 percent against 23.7
percent, so the model is reproducing a real difference rather than inventing
one. But the error rates do not follow along evenly. A senior who was not
going to leave has a 38.5 percent chance of being offered a discount, against
18.4 percent for a non-senior. Positive predictive value, by contrast, is
nearly equal at 0.595 and 0.545.

That combination is not a bug to be fixed. When two groups have different base
rates, equal PPV and equal FPR cannot both hold. Choosing which to equalise is
a value judgement. Equal PPV says a flag should mean the same thing whoever
receives it. Equal FPR says no group should be bothered more often for no
reason. Here the model happens to deliver roughly the first and clearly not
the second, and nobody made that choice deliberately.

Customers with dependents are affected in the opposite direction. Only 55.1
percent of churning customers with dependents are caught, against 77.5 percent
of those without. If the retention offer is worth having, families are the
group least likely to be offered it.

The threshold makes these gaps worse, not better. At 0.354 the senior
selection rate rises to 77.3 percent, so three quarters of senior customers
are flagged and exactly half of those flags are wrong.

None of this is visible in the application. The metrics endpoint returns one
global set of numbers. A user can see that precision is 0.558 and cannot see
that the false positive rate differs by a factor of two across age groups.


4. WHAT THE MODEL RELIES ON

Permutation importance, scored with roc_auc so the numbers do not shift when
the threshold moves:

  tenure            +0.2435
  TotalCharges      +0.0407
  Contract          +0.0308
  everything else   below 0.005

  Dependents        +0.0008
  SeniorCitizen     +0.0005
  gender            -0.0000
  Partner           -0.0001

The coefficients agree. The three largest are Contract_Two year at -0.595,
Contract_Month-to-month at +0.593 and InternetService_Fiber optic at +0.309.

So the model reads how long someone has been a customer and what contract they
are on. The four demographic columns contribute essentially nothing, and
removing them would change almost no predictions.

That is reassuring and it does not resolve section 3. The group differences
exist anyway, because tenure and contract type are correlated with age and
family status. Deleting the protected attributes would leave the disparity
intact while removing the ability to measure it. "The model does not use age"
and "the model treats age groups differently" are both true here, which is
exactly why fairness has to be measured on outcomes rather than argued from
the feature list.


5. USABILITY

The API takes features as they appear in the data. Contract=Month-to-month
and tenure=12 work directly, because the fitted pipeline carries the encoder
and the imputer. The reference implementation we were shown in class requires
pre-standardised floats such as age=0.970798, which means a caller cannot use
it without the training set's mean and standard deviation. Moving that work
behind the API removes a whole class of silent error.

The /schema endpoint makes the service self-describing, and the frontend
builds its form from it rather than hardcoding 19 fields. Errors are explicit:
an unseen category returns 400 naming the offending field, rather than being
silently encoded as all zeros and producing a confident wrong answer.

Against that:

- Nineteen fields is a long form. The defaults are the dataset median and
  mode, and that default customer scores 62.4 percent, above the threshold.
  Anyone who opens the app and presses Predict without editing anything gets
  "Churn risk" for a customer they never described. That is a misleading
  default and I would change it.
- The threshold slider takes effect immediately, with no confirmation, and is
  shared by all users.
- There is no authentication, no logging, and the backend runs on Flask's
  development server in debug mode.

So it is easy to use and not ready to be used.


6. TRUSTWORTHINESS

The model is honest about being mediocre, if anyone reads the numbers. At
threshold 0.584, 436 of the 987 flagged customers were not going to churn and
197 of the 748 real churners were missed. Roughly two in five flags are wrong
and one in four churners is never caught. ROC AUC is 0.8415, which is useful
for ranking and nowhere near good enough for an automatic decision.

Three specific reasons to discount the reported numbers:

The threshold is chosen by maximising F1 on the test set, and the application
then reports test metrics at that threshold. That is circular. Choosing it
out-of-fold on the training data instead gives 0.501, and the test F1 at that
threshold is 0.6290 rather than 0.6352. The headline is about 0.006
optimistic. Small, but the app presents it as an unbiased result and it is
not. Permutation importance is computed on the same test set.

Everything rests on one 60/40 split with one seed. The previous assignment
found four models within 0.012 of each other on this data and the ranking
reordered between cross validation and the test set. Calling logistic
regression the best model is not supported, it tied.

The optimiser converged after 1866 of the 2000 iterations allowed. It finished,
but not with much room, and that is a consequence of the winning configuration
using no feature scaling.

None of this reaches the user. The API returns a probability to sixteen
decimal places, which implies a precision the model does not have.


7. WHAT I WOULD WANT BEFORE THIS WENT ANYWHERE NEAR CUSTOMERS

- Choose the threshold out-of-fold, so the reported metrics are not measured
  on the data the threshold was fitted to.
- Report metrics per group in the API, not just globally, so the disparities
  in section 3 are visible to whoever is moving the slider.
- Log threshold changes with a user and a reason, and make the threshold
  per-client rather than global.
- Write down what a flag actually triggers, and re-run section 3 with that in
  mind, because whether these gaps are harmful depends entirely on it.
- A production server, authentication, and debug mode off.

The first two are the ones that matter. The rest is engineering.
